---
title: Online vs. Over-the-Board
summary: Same game, two social worlds — what transfers, what doesn't, and the etiquette that separates a pleasant club night from an awkward one.
---

Online chess and over-the-board (OTB) chess are the same game played in two
different social worlds. Skills transfer better than you'd expect. *Habits*
transfer worse — several online behaviors are quietly illegal or rude in
person, and several OTB skills don't even exist online. This chapter is the
translation layer.

## What transfers (good news)

- Everything tactical: checks, captures, threats, forks, pins, back-rank
  patterns. The position doesn't know which table it's on.
- The opening principles, exactly as written in chapter 3.
- Blunder recovery, phase timing, annotation — all board-agnostic.

## What doesn't transfer (honest news)

- **You can't see the whole board.** Online, the interface holds your hand:
  pieces highlight when selected, last move is glowy, illegal moves refuse
  to happen. At a physical board you must *see* the diagonals — including
  the pinned ones — and illegal moves are your problem.
- **The clock is a piece of furniture.** Pressing it with the same hand that
  moved (compulsory in rated play), remembering to press at all, and
  writing moves mid-thought are all new motor skills. Expect two weeks of
  feeling clumsy. Everyone did.
- **No en-passant enforcement for free.** The rule exists either way, but
  online it fires at one click; across a table it must be claimed in the
  moment or (usually) it's gone for that move.

## Etiquette differences that matter on club night

**At the table, always:**

- **Shake hands.** Before the game ("good luck") and after ("thanks for the
  game") — win, lose, or draw. It's a real social norm at most clubs, not a
  formality you can skip because you're nervous. Post-game handshake comes
  *after* you make sure the clocks are off.
- **Phones away and silenced.** Not on the table face-down, not on vibrate
  in a pocket you'll check. In the bag, by the coats.
- **Talk only about the game's public facts.** It's fine to say "check," to
  offer a draw, to resign, or to correct a scoresheet. It's not fine to
  narrate your ideas, ask your opponent what they're doing, comment on the
  quality of play (in either direction), or chat — unless your *opponent*
  opens the conversation, and even then, keep it light and brief.
- **Resign when it's clearly lost.** Turning the board over with a "you've
  got it" and a handshake is normal and even kind. Playing out a completely
  lost position for forty more minutes to "see what happens" is the least
  liked habit in club chess. (One exception: in a clearly drawn-looking
  endgame, playing on a modest amount is fine — nobody grudges that.)
- **Both players keep score.** Writing moves isn't optional bookkeeping for
  them; it's part of the game and your best annotation material (chapter 5).
- **Touch move rules at the board.** Chapter 2 covers this; at the club it
  actually gets enforced, gently.
- **Sit still, mostly.** Fidgeting is fine, standing up and hovering behind
  your *own* chair to breathe is fine, but walking around, leaning over
  *other* boards, or staring hard at your opponent's face is frowned on.

**Online, some of the above relax and others sharpen:**

- Resigning on a clearly lost game is still good form (though taking your
  full clock time before resigning is fine — the clock is the clock).
- Drawing repeatedly to farm rating points, and stalling in completely lost
  positions to wait out an opponent's clock, are the online equivalents of
  bad form. Just resign.
- Takebacks that the interface allowed are forgiven on both sides online;
  at the board they don't exist, so it's moot.

## A five-step arrival script for your first night

1. Arrive 15 minutes early. Meet whoever runs the room and say the words:
   "Hi, I'm a new player — this is my first night. What should I know?"
2. Watch one game in progress if you can, at a gentle distance, until you're
   called.
3. When paired, handshake, set your clock and scoresheet (chapter 11 covers
   the checklist), and play *your* game only.
4. After the handshake at the end, spend five minutes with the TD or a
   friendly regular: result, what happened, scoresheet read.
5. Leave before fatigue decides the rating of your memories — first nights
   should end on purpose, on your terms, at a time you chose.
