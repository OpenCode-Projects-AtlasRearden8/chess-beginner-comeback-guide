The Chess Beginner Comeback Guide

The Ten-Game Starter Plan

Your first ten games, each with one job — a two-month runway from knowing the rules to playing over-the-board like you belong.

Ten games is the shortest honest curriculum. One game teaches you almost nothing clean; a hundred is the salary of a coach. But ten games, each with one deliberate focus, produces a player: someone who knows their own blunders, manages their clock, and doesn't get flustered by a real room. Here is the plan — spread it over roughly two months at the gentle-week pace (chapter 9).

Format notes: play 1–6 online slow (15+10 style pace unless you have a better local option), games 7–8 casual over-the-board if a human is willing, and games 9–10 at or near a club setting. Record and annotate every game (chapter 5) — the plan runs on the margins, not the results.

Games 1–2: see the clock, castle early

Focus one: play the opening by principles only. e4 or d4 map from chapter 3, castling by move 8, no queen sorties. That's the entire job. Result doesn't matter. Don't even look at the evaluation — you're installing the map.

Focus two (or the second game): notice your clock. Using the phase budget (chapter 6), roughly mark where your opening ended. No management yet — just a feel for where your minutes actually go. First honest data point: most adults burn their opening budget by move 10 and don't know it.

Games 3–4: C.C.T. every move

The whole game is: before every move — checks, captures, threats, theirs first (chapter 4). You'll fail it repeatedly and that's exactly the point: the failures cost pennies at this stage and create the habit. Also expect: these games feel cramped and slow. That's what thinking feels like before it becomes automatic.

Games 5–6: the middlegame plan

After castling, pick one middlegame plan and play toward it for the rest of the game, no matter what develops. The beginner menu, pick one per game: (a) put a rook on an open/half-open file with a pawn target on it, (b) trade into a pawn endgame with clean targets, (c) attack the king if, and only if, more of your pieces are developed than theirs. You will lose games 5 and/or 6 in new ways. That's the design: the losses cost you a lesson you can see arriving.

Games 7–8: paper and a person

Play casually across a physical board with someone you like, using a printed scoresheet (the companion scoresheet pack exists for this), at 10 or 15 minutes a side or untimed if that's friendlier. Both records kept. Count this as OTB rehearsal: clock built into the table, touch-move in the room, scoresheet in your handwriting. Afterward, annotate as usual, plus write two lines on what felt physically different from online.

Games 9–10: rehearsal for the room

Either your club's beginners' night (checklist in chapter 11) or a self-run simulation: a quiet café, a real scoresheet, a real clock, coffee, phone in the bag, handshake before and after, twenty-minute C.C.T. discipline, and the full post-game five-minute debrief out loud with yourself or your partner. After game 10, write the review on the blank page below the last scoresheet of the batch: what improved, what still leaks, what the drills for the next four weeks should target (chapter 9).

The scoreboard that matters

Not wins. At the end of ten games, you're keeping track of these five marks — count how many you hit, aim to nail them all before club night:

  • ☐ Castled by move 8 in at least half the games without being asked
  • ☐ C.C.T. (theirs first) became a reflex you miss when you skip it
  • ☐ Noticed your clock in real time once the middlegame started
  • ☐ Two annotated margins you'd show another beginner as useful
  • ☐ One OTB game on paper where scoresheet and clock both felt normal

Three or more is club-ready. Four or five means club night is mostly rehearsal. And if you've got 1 of 5 — the plan still works, it just runs ten more games before the club. That's not failure; that's the syllabus being honest. Whatever the count, the games you played on the way are the only reason you'd ever have been ready.
