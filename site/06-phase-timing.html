The Chess Beginner Comeback Guide

Phase Timing

How to spend minutes at the clock in the opening, middlegame, and endgame — so you stop losing on time and start losing (and winning) on the board.

Adults lose more beginner games to the clock than to combinations. Not because adults can't think fast — because nobody ever told them the game has three phases with different speeds, and each one costs a different kind of minute. This chapter gives you a budget.

The three phases

The opening (roughly moves 1–10). This is the scheduled part. Your ten principles (chapter 3) apply to nearly every position, so you should almost never spend more than a minute or two per side here. The right thought per opening move is short: "develop, castle, or claim center?"

The middlegame (moves 10–30, give or take). The weird, specific, different-every-game part. This is where your deep thinking belongs, and where the C.C.T. scan earns its keep, position by position. Budget your biggest chunks of time here.

The endgame (when the queens and most pieces leave). Simple in shape, unforgiving in accuracy — a rook endgame can be technically drawn with four seconds and lost with thirty. It asks for calm, deliberate, slow moves, which is a problem if you've already burned your clock.

The beginner's budget, per game

Take your clock time (in minutes) and divide it like this, aiming from move 10 onward rather than by stopwatch:

Phase Share of your clock Why
Opening ~10% principles run it; don't major in the minor key
Middlegame ~60% the phase that decides who wins, and needs real thought
Endgame ~30% (often unused) small, careful moves; many games end before here

So on a 30-minute game: about 3 minutes for the opening, 18 banked for the middlegame, 9 reserve. On 15: roughly 1.5 / 9 / 4.5. Nobody's asking you to time this — it's a shape, not a stopwatch. If you reach move 15 with your middlegame budget still intact, you're doing it right.

What "cover your clock" looks like in practice

Time trouble at beginner level comes from exactly two habits, and both are fixable:

  • Over-thinking the opening. You're not solving anything there; the map (chapter 3) is. If your first ten moves cost more than 15% of your clock, your opening is where you're leaking.
  • Under-thinking cheap moves. Not every move deserves thought. A forced recapture, the only legal move, a completely forced check sequence — play them quickly and confidently, and bank the seconds for the genuinely branching moments. Beginners hoard time on obvious moves and panic-spend it on hard ones, backwards from how it should go.

Practical rhythm: if a position is obviously simple — forced sequence, automatic recapture, pure development — make that move in under 15 seconds. If it's a choice with staying power — two good plans, a sacrifice, a trade decision — you're allowed minutes. That single distinction, applied roughly, fixes most time trouble.

Increments, plain English

Many club and online games add a few seconds back after each move (15+10 = fifteen minutes, ten seconds per move). Increment quietly does two things: it means deep-thought positions can take real time without flag risk, and it makes flag-falls in won positions embarrassing rather than tragic. When you have a choice, prefer increment formats — they fit the way adults think.

The one-line shape to remember

Move fast through the scheduled part. Slow down where the game gets personal. Keep reserve for when it gets small and exact. If you do nothing else from this chapter, remember the middlegame is where your thinking minutes belong — and it's also the phase this guide's habits (C.C.T., the blunder ritual) are built for.
