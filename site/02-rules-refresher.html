The Chess Beginner Comeback Guide

Rules Refresher

The rules you know, the rules you half-know, and the ones that only come up in person — ten quiet minutes that make club night feel normal.

You could skip this chapter and still survive club night. But there are four rules and two habits that adults self-taught on apps often miss, and they all surface in person, out loud, in front of a table of people. Ten minutes here makes that moment boring, which is the goal.

The rules you probably know

Move and capture, check and checkmate, the pieces' moves, pawn promotion. If any of those are shaky, play five casual online games at slow speed and you have them. Don't grind more than that — the rules firm up fastest when they're loading-bearing inside a real game.

The four that trip up online players

Touch move. Once you touch a piece deliberately, you must move it — and if you touch an opponent's piece, you must capture with it or with the piece that legally attacks it. If a piece is badly placed or you brushed it while adjusting, say "j'adoube" (French for "I adjust") before you touch it, and you may re-center it without consequence. In club play the rule is enforced by your opponent, politely. On a screen it doesn't exist, which is why your hand keeps hovering over pieces at the board for the first month.

En passant. When a pawn advances two squares beside an enemy pawn that could have captured it on its one-step version, that enemy pawn may capture "in passing" — immediately, on that very move only. It happens rarely and always feels like a magic trick the first time. A glance at this diagram is worth a paragraph:

Rank Before After (Black's 1... move)
5th White pawn just jumped d2–d4; Black pawn on e5 Black plays exd3, removing the pawn on d4

The capturing pawn lands behind where the jumped pawn passed. If you forget it exists, you'll meet it once per season. If you know it exists, you only ever feel briefly confused. That's a good trade.

Stalemate is a draw. A player with no legal moves who is not in check is not mated — the game is a draw, half a point each. Don't reach for it in one jump; consolidate material, then let it happen.

Castling's fine print. Both king and rook must be untouched, no pieces between them, the king may not castle out of, through, or into check. King moves two squares toward the rook; rook hops over. That's all the fine print there is.

Over-the-board extras that surprise adults

  • You keep score. Most rated over-the-board games require both players to write every move — your moves and theirs — on a scoresheet. This is the single biggest adjustment from online. Our companion pack, the Free Chess Scoresheets (ships as its own project), has regulation-style printable sheets and a post-game reflection page; the notation habit is covered step by step in Your First Games.
  • Clocks are physical. You press your clock after moving. Forgetting to press it just means your time keeps draining, a mistake that fixes itself with one bad experience. Some club games have an increment: a few seconds added back after every move.
  • Plain talking. Announce check by word or silence — both are fine. What you shouldn't do is narrate your plans aloud mid-game or comment on your opponent's choices. See Online vs. Over-the-Board for the full etiquette.

The threefold and fifty-move draws, briefly

If the same position, with the same player to move and same legal options, occurs three times, either player may claim a draw. If fifty moves pass with no pawn move and no capture, either player may claim a draw. Know these exist; don't track them. An opponent or arbiter will handle it if it ever matters, and at club level it essentially never does.

A five-minute drill to close the chapter

Set the board up. Do each of these once, slowly, narrating to yourself:

  1. Castle kingside as White; then castles queenside — notice the king lands on c1, not d1.
  2. Move a pawn so en passant becomes legal, then take it with a second pawn.
  3. Set up any position where a knight is about to be promoted — then promote to a ... knight, just to see it happen (underpromoting is legal; a queen is almost always correct, but you should see the trick once on purpose).

When all three feel unremarkable, you're done. That's the entire technical load this manual will put on you.
